# Source: RFC 6781 — DNSSEC Operational Practices, Version 2

> **Raw file**: `raw/dns-sec-rfc/RFC 6781 DNSSEC Operational Practices, Version 2.md`
> **Source URL**: https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6781
> **Authors**: O. Kolkman, W. Mekking (NLnet Labs), R. Gieben (SIDN Labs)
> **Published**: December 2012
> **Type**: IETF Informational RFC — operational guidance for zone administrators
> **Obsoletes**: RFC 4641
> **Coverage axes**: Key rollover (Axis 5), security operations, timing theory

---

## Summary

The canonical IETF operational reference for DNSSEC zone administrators. Defines formal timing terminology (Refresh Period, Re-Sign Period, key effectivity period), specifies the Pre-Publish ZSK and Double-KSK rollover procedures (which BIND 9's `dnssec-policy` implements), and covers emergency rollover procedures, stand-by keys, DNS operator change procedures, and NSEC3 parameter guidance. Published 2012 — some NSEC3 guidance is superseded by RFC 9276 (2022).

---

## Key Claims

### Timing Terminology (Formal Definitions)

1. **Signature validity period** — Duration between the `Signature Inception` and `Signature Expiration` timestamps in an RRSIG record.

2. **Signature publication period** — Duration the signature is actually published in the zone. May be shorter than validity period (if refreshed before expiry).

3. **Key effectivity period** — Duration from earliest RRSIG inception to latest RRSIG expiration for all signatures made with a key. Spans multiple validity periods.

4. **Re-Sign Period** — How often a signer visits the zone to refresh signatures approaching expiry.

5. **Refresh Period** — The window before RRSIG expiration within which signatures must be refreshed. Must be larger than the Re-Sign Period.

6. **Relationship**: `Re-Sign Period < Refresh Period < Signature Validity Period`. The gap between Refresh Period and validity period determines how long an operator has to respond to signing failure before signatures expire.

7. **SOA expiry recommendation** — The SOA expire field should be approximately 1/3 to 1/4 of the signature validity period, so that secondary servers fail to resolve the zone before signatures expire. This prevents secondaries from serving Bogus (signature-expired) data.

### Rollover Procedures (Canonical Reference)

8. **ZSK Pre-Publish rollover (4 stages)**:
   - Stage 1 (initial): current ZSK active
   - Stage 2 (new DNSKEY): new ZSK published alongside old; old ZSK still signing
   - Stage 3 (new RRSIGs): new ZSK starts signing; both ZSKs + new RRSIGs in zone
   - Stage 4 (DNSKEY removal): old ZSK removed; new ZSK only
   - **Wait**: 1 Maximum Zone TTL between stages 2→3; 1 TTL between 3→4
   - **Advantage**: Zone size doesn't double

9. **ZSK Double-Signature rollover**: Both ZSKs sign everything simultaneously → wait 1 TTL → remove old ZSK. Simpler but zone doubles in size during transition.

10. **KSK Double-Signature rollover (1 parent interaction)**:
    - Add new KSK; both KSKs sign DNSKEY RRset
    - Update DS in parent to point to new KSK
    - After old DS TTL expires: remove old KSK
    
11. **Single-Type (CSK) rollover** = essentially the same as Double-DS KSK rollover. New DNSKEY published → DS submitted to parent → after DS propagates → swap signing to new key → remove old DNSKEY. Requires parent interaction.

### Emergency Key Rollover

12. **Two choices when KSK is compromised**: Keep chain of trust intact OR break it.

    **Keep chain intact** (attacker still has the compromised key during transition):
    1. Introduce new KSK; keep compromised KSK in zone
    2. Lower DNSKEY TTL; sign with short validity period (expires ~when new DS appears in parent)
    3. Upload new DS to parent
    4. Follow normal KSK rollover procedure
    5. Remove compromised KSK only after new DS is in parent
    
    **Break chain of trust** (zone goes Bogus or Insecure during transition):
    - Option A (Bogus): Replace KSK immediately → zone appears Bogus until new DS published in parent
    - Option B (Insecure): Remove DS from parent entirely → zone goes Insecure → re-key → re-enroll with new DS

13. **ZSK compromise is less severe**: No parent interaction needed. Roll ZSK immediately. Zone still at risk until old RRSIG TTLs expire.

14. **Stand-by keys**: Pre-published keys that can be activated quickly in an emergency.
    - Stand-by ZSK: publish its DNSKEY in zone (not yet signing)
    - Stand-by KSK: get its DS published in parent zone (pre-positioned)
    - Recovery: activate stand-by keys → sign with them → remove old keys after TTL expiry

### DNS Operator Change

15. **Cooperating operators** (best case): Losing operator pre-publishes gaining operator's ZSK and KSK. After propagation, re-delegation updates NS and DS. After old records expire, old operator's keys are removed.

16. **Non-cooperating operators** (only viable path): Zone must go **Insecure** during the transition. Registry removes DS → zone goes insecure → re-delegate to new operator → new operator re-signs → new DS submitted.

### NSEC3 Parameters (2012 guidance — partially superseded)

17. **Iterations** — RFC 6781 suggested 100 iterations as "sufficiently costly yet not excessive." **⚠️ SUPERSEDED by RFC 9276 (2022)**: 0 iterations is now recommended. See [[dnssec-internals]].

18. **Salt** — Provides no long-term protection benefit once an attacker captures zone data; primarily forces re-computation on salt change. Modern guidance (RFC 9276): empty salt.

19. **Opt-Out** — Only useful for large TLD zones with many insecure delegations. Not recommended for regular zones.

20. **NSEC vs NSEC3 choice** — NSEC3's anti-enumeration benefit is irrelevant for small zones (www, mail, apex records only) because an attacker can trivially guess record names. NSEC is preferred for small zones.

## Direct Quotes Worth Preserving

> "Zone administrators who are in the process of rolling their keys have to take into account the fact that data published in previous versions of their zone still lives in caches."

> "The Re-Sign Period must be smaller than the Refresh Period in order for zone data to be signed in a timely fashion."

> "We suggest that the SOA expiration timer be approximately one third or a quarter of the signature validity period."

> "The only viable operation for the registrant [when the losing operator doesn't cooperate] is to have his zone go Insecure for the duration of the change."

---

## ⚠️ Contradiction: NSEC3 Iterations

RFC 6781 (§5.3.2) recommends up to 100 NSEC3 iterations as a reasonable choice. **RFC 9276 (August 2022)** supersedes this, recommending 0 iterations because modern GPU-based attacks make any iteration count ineffective as a defense, while each iteration adds server-side CPU cost.

Current wiki guidance in [[dnssec-internals]] follows RFC 9276. The RFC 6781 recommendation is now obsolete for this parameter.
